The rule of product

N = c1 × c2 × ⋯ × ck

Build the thing you are counting as a sequence of decisions, so that every possible thing gets made exactly once. If decision 1 has c1 options, decision 2 has c2 options whatever you chose in decision 1, and so on, then the total is the product.

A lunch is one of five sandwiches, one of two drinks and one of three fruits. Each sandwich starts a group of 2 drink choices, so 5 × 2 = 10 sandwich-and-drink pairs; each pair starts a group of 3 fruits, so 10 × 3 = 30 lunches. A tree shows the branching, but once you know the branch counts you don't need to draw every leaf.

The options at a step may change depending on earlier choices. Only their number has to stay the same.

From class

Outfits (Example 1.1). Four shirts, three pants, two shoes: 4 × 3 = 12 shirt-and-pants pairs, each with 2 shoe options, 24 outfits.

Worked numbers

A dinner is one of 10 appetizers, 5 mains and 3 desserts: 10 × 5 × 3 = 150 dinners. A three-digit number built left to right has 9 × 10 × 10 = 900 possibilities; built right to left it's 10 × 10 × 9, the same 900.

Columns can be decisions too. If m + n = 1492 with no carrying, each column of the sum splits on its own: the ones digit 2 is 0 + 2, 1 + 1 or 2 + 0, which is 3 ways. A digit d splits into d + 1 ordered pairs.

Restrictions change the options

9 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 90,000

A number is a list of digits, so build it digit by digit. The only rule for an ordinary five-digit number is that the first digit can't be zero: 04372 is really the four-digit number 4372. So the first place has 9 options and each other place has 10.

Check it a second way: five-digit numbers run from 10,000 to 99,999, and 99,999 − 10,000 + 1 = 90,000.

A restriction like "only digits below 4" changes the list at every place. Two-digit numbers using only 0, 1, 2, 3: the first place is 1, 2 or 3 (3 options), the second is 0, 1, 2 or 3 (4 options), so 3 × 4 = 12. Write the allowed list for each place, then count it.

From class

Only odd digits (Example 1.3). Every place uses 1, 3, 5, 7, 9. None of them is zero, so the first place isn't special: 5⁵ = 3125.

Worked numbers

Three-digit numbers with only even digits: first place 2, 4, 6, 8 (4 options, zero is even but can't lead), then 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 twice (5 each): 4 × 5 × 5 = 100.

A license plate is not a number, so zero may go first: four digit spots give 10⁴ = 10,000, not 9 × 10³.

Decide the most restricted thing first

4 × 23 = 32

You choose the order of your decisions. If one rule is hard to track, settle it before anything else.

Four beads in a row, each red, white or black, with exactly one red bead. Going left to right fails: a later bead has 3 options or 2 depending on whether red was already used, so there's no single number to multiply by. Making the first bead red fails too; it misses every row with red somewhere else. Instead decide which bead is red first (4 options), then color the rest white or black (2 each): 4 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32.

You can even build a three-digit number right to left: 10 × 10 × 9 gives the same 900.

From class

Family seating (Problem 2.1). The driver's seat is restricted: 2 parents. The other three people fill the other seats: 3 × 2 × 1. Total 2 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 12.

Worked numbers

Two boys at the ends of a row, three girls in the middle: seat the boys first (2 ways), then the girls (3! = 6): 12.

Letters that must sit together: glue them into one block and choose the block's position first. Two letters side by side among six characters can start at positions 1 to 5, so 5 positions.

Permutations and factorials

n! = n × (n − 1) × ⋯ × 2 × 1,   0! = 1

A permutation is an arrangement, an ordering, of a group of things. For different objects the choices shrink by one at every position. Five friends in a line: 5 choices for the front, then 4, 3, 2, 1, so 5! = 120 lines.

The friend in second place depends on who went first, but there are always four candidates. Different options, same number of them, so the rule of product works. Try a couple of sample paths to make sure nothing behaves differently.

Zero objects can be arranged in exactly one way: the empty arrangement, like an empty desk. That is why 0! = 1, which also keeps n! = n × (n − 1)! true at n = 1.

From class

A, B, C (Example 1.4). ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA: 3 × 2 × 1 = 6.

Worked numbers

M, A, T, H: 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24. Check: 6 arrangements start with each of the four letters, 4 × 6 = 24.

Useful values: 1! = 1, 2! = 2, 3! = 6, 4! = 24, 5! = 120, 6! = 720, 7! = 5040.

Shrinking sets

6 × 5 × 4 × 3 = 360

Four-letter codes from the letters A to F with no letter used twice: 6 options for the first letter, 5 for the second, 4, then 3. That's 360 codes.

Digits work the same way with one twist: the first digit can't be zero, but the second can. Two-digit numbers with different digits: 9 options first, then 10 − 1 = 9, so 9 × 9 = 81. It looks like a factorial but isn't one: the first place is special, and we stop early.

From class

Five different digits (Example 1.5). 9 × 9 × 8 × 7 × 6 = 27,216.

Worked numbers

Three-digit numbers with different digits: 9 × 9 × 8 = 648. Four-digit: 9 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 4536. The second factor is 9 both times: zero comes back in, and one digit is used up.

Overcounting and repeated letters

arrangements = n!m1! · m2! ⋯ mr!

Overcounting is counting some or all things more than once. For T, O, O a letter-by-letter tree fails: after T there is one kind of letter left, after O there are two, so there's no single multiplier.

Instead pretend the O's are different, a blue O and a red O. Three different letters: 3! = 6 arrangements. Every real word (TOO, OTO, OOT) appears exactly twice, blue first or red first, so there are 6 ÷ 2 = 3. Divide only when everything is counted the same number of times.

With three identical letters the overcount factor is the number of ways to order three colored copies, 3! = 6, not 3. For B, E, E, E: 4! ÷ 3! = 24 ÷ 6 = 4 (BEEE, EBEE, EEBE, EEEB). With two repeated groups the orderings combine by the rule of product: A, A, B, B gives 4! ÷ (2! × 2!) = 6.

Cancel before multiplying: 6!/4! = 6 × 5 = 30.

From class

A, A, B, C and A, A, A, B, C (Examples 1.6, 1.7). 4!/2! = 12 and 5!/3! = 5 × 4 = 20. A student in class suggested dividing 120 by 3 for the three A's; the factor is 3! = 6, the orderings of the colored A's.

Worked numbers

HELLO: 5!/2! = 120/2 = 60. GREENER: 7!/(3! · 2!) = 5040/12 = 420. MISSISSIPPI: 11!/(4! · 4! · 2!) = 34,650, and the class left it in factorial form.

Overcounting also shows up without letters. If each of 8 teams plays 14 conference games, 8 × 14 = 112 counts every game twice, once for each team in it: 56 games.

Identical groups

8!2!

Eight different books go into two stacks of four, one stack on each of two labeled shelves. Order inside a stack matters, so filling the shelves one slot at a time is just arranging all eight books: 8!.

Now put the stacks into two identical boxes instead. Any finished pair of stacks can be put on labeled shelves in 2! ways, and those now look the same. Divide by 2!. Don't divide by 4! for each stack: swapping books inside a stack still changes the stack.

From class

Coins in tubes (board example). Fifteen distinct coins, three stacks of five. Labeled tubes: 15!. Identical tubes: 15!/3!. There's no division by (5!)³.

Worked numbers

A smaller case you can check by hand: six coins, three stacks of two. Labeled tubes: 6! = 720. Identical tubes: 720/3! = 120. A computer listing agrees with both.

Same number of choices, different choices

3 × 23 = 24

A four-letter sequence of R, G, B where no letter appears twice in a row. After R the next letter is G or B; after G it's R or B. The next letters depend on the last one, but there are always two of them. So 3 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 24. That's all the rule of product needs.

From class

Good words (Problem 2.2). After A: A or C. After B: A or B. After C: B or C. Seven letters: 3 × 2⁶ = 192.

Worked numbers

Ten kids each get 1, 2 or 3 candies, and no two neighbors may total 4. After 1 the next can't be 3; after 2, not 2; after 3, not 1. Always two choices: 3 × 2⁹ = 1536.

Removing order to count sets

5 × 4 × 33! = 10

A set has no first, second or third member. Picking a team of three from five kids one at a time gives 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 ordered picks, but each team was picked once for every order of its three members: 3! = 6 times. So there are 60 ÷ 6 = 10 teams.

Build with order, then divide by the number of orders.

From class

Complementary cards (Problem 2.3). Any two different cards force the third, feature by feature. Ordered draws 27 × 26 × 1 = 702; each set is drawn in 3! = 6 orders, so 117 sets.

Worked numbers

An increasing number like 1368 is fixed by which digits it uses; the order comes for free. So counting increasing numbers means counting groups of digits from 1 to 9: each digit in or out, 2⁹, minus the empty group, 511.

Pick grids: when does order matter?

pairs = n × (n − 1)2

To count pairs from a group, build a grid. Put the first pick down the side and the second pick across the top. Four people, A, B, C, D, make a 4 × 4 grid: 16 boxes, and every box is one way to say "first I pick this person, then that one."

Ask three questions, in order.

  1. How many boxes? 4 × 4 = 16.
  2. Which boxes aren't allowed? The diagonal picks the same person twice (A then A). Cross out those 4: 12 left.
  3. Are any two boxes the same thing? Point at AB, then at BA. If the order doesn't matter, they're the same pair, and every teal box has a grey twin across the diagonal. So 12 boxes are really 6 pairs: 12 ÷ 2 = 6.

Question 3 is the whole idea. If the answer is no (a home game and an away game, a red card and a black card, a captain and a vice-captain) there are no twins and nothing to divide. If the answer is yes (handshakes, teams, hands of cards) divide by 2.

Point at any grey box to light up its twin.

From the unit test

From the unit test. Splitting six students into a group of 2 and a group of 4 has the same answer as just choosing the 2: naming the pair names the four. The grid below works it out.

Worked numbers

Handshakes among 12 people: 12 × 11 = 132 boxes off the diagonal, twins everywhere, 66 handshakes. Games between 8 teams playing home and away: 8 × 7 = 56 boxes, no twins, 56 games.

The pick grid

pairs = n × (n − 1)2

This is the picture to draw whenever a problem picks two things and you're not sure whether to divide. It comes straight from the group-of-2 problem on the unit test: Ms. Q splits Lizzie, Grogg, Alex, Winnie, Ralph and Fiona into a group of 2 and a group of 4.

Building the grid

  1. Set it up. Write the six students down the side (the first student picked) and again across the top (the second student picked). Every box is one way to say "first I pick this person, then I pick that person."
  2. How many boxes are there? 6 × 6 = 36.
  3. Why are the × boxes not allowed? You can't pick Lizzie and then pick Lizzie again. That removes the 6 boxes down the diagonal, leaving 30.
  4. This is the moment that matters. Point at the LG box, then at the GL box, and ask: are those two different groups? They aren't. It's Lizzie and Grogg either way. Every teal box has a grey twin on the other side of the diagonal, so the 30 boxes are really 15 groups, each showing up twice. 30 ÷ 2 = 15.

If he can answer step 4 himself, he understands the whole idea. That one question, "is this the same thing written backwards?", is what separates a problem where you divide from one where you multiply and stop.

Two surprises are worth saying out loud. First, the group of 4 costs nothing: if the problem had just said "choose 2 students" the grid would be exactly the same picture, and the answer still 15. Naming the 2 already names the 4. Second, you can check from the other side: choosing 4 of the 6 also gives 15, because every group of 4 leaves exactly one pair behind. The two counts list the same 15 splits from opposite ends.

Same grid, different answers

Draw the grid, then ask the three questions. The answers change from problem to problem, and so does what you divide by.

Home and away games: no twins

Problem 12 counts games between 8 teams who play each other twice, home and away. Team 1 at home to team 2 is a different game from team 2 at home to team 1, so every box off the diagonal is its own game: 56. The 8 × 14 = 112 trap comes from counting each game from both teams' sides, which is a different grid.

Handshakes: twins everywhere

One red card, one black card: no diagonal, no twins

When the two picks come from separate piles, the grid is a rectangle. No box picks the same card twice, and no box has a twin, because a red card can never be in the black column. Dividing by 2 here out of habit gives 338, which is wrong.

A spade and a face card: twins only in one corner

This is the hardest one, and the grid makes it easy to see. The two piles overlap in three cards, J♠, Q♠ and K♠. Only there can a box pick the same card twice (the three × boxes), and only there can two boxes be the same hand (J♠ + Q♠ appears once with J♠ as the spade, once with Q♠ as the spade). 156 − 3 − 3 = 150. The tempting wrong answers are 156 (no corrections) and 153 (only the × boxes).

Tricky bits

right count = count with extra distinctionstimes each thing was counted

Each trap comes from a specific problem in Problem Set 3, with the wrong answer it produces.

1. Letting zero lead

Wrong answer: 1000 · Problem 1

Counting every digit string 000–999. It's tempting because each place "has 10 digits". A number can't start with 0.

2. Forgetting zero is even

Wrong answer: 64 · Problem 2

Using 2, 4, 6, 8 in every place. Zero is even, and it's allowed in the middle and last places.

3. Letting zero lead, even digits

Wrong answer: 125 · Problem 2

Five even digits in every place, including a leading zero.

4. Three colors, then "× 1"

Wrong answer: 81 · Problem 3

Giving the first four squares 3 colors each and forcing the last. A later square has 2 or 3 options depending on earlier choices, so there's no single multiplier.

5. Yellow in one fixed spot

Wrong answer: 16 · Problem 3

Painting the first square yellow and coloring the rest. It misses every coloring with yellow elsewhere.

6. Zero banned in the middle

Wrong answer: 504 · Problem 6

Using 9, 8, 7. Zero can't lead, but it can come second or third, so the second place has 9 options, not 8.

7. Dividing by the number of repeats

Wrong answer: 840 · Problem 8

Dividing 5040 by 3 × 2 instead of 3! × 2!. The overcount is the number of orderings of the repeated letters.

8. Only one repeat group divided out

Wrong answer: 2520 · Problem 8

Dividing by 2! for the R's and forgetting the E's (or the other way round gives 840).

9. Adding the factorials underneath

Wrong answer: 798336 · Problem 9

Writing 11!/(4! + 4! + 2!). The orderings of the groups combine by the rule of product, so they multiply.

10. Counting each game from both sides

Wrong answer: 144 · Problem 12

8 teams × 14 conference games = 112 counts every conference game twice. Non-conference games are counted once, correctly.

11. Treating the candies as free

Wrong answer: 59049 · Problem 13

3¹⁰ ignores the neighbor rule. The rule removes exactly one option for every kid after the first.

12. Every pair of ends works

Wrong answer: 90 · Problem 14

9 first digits × 10 last digits. When one end is odd and the other even, the average isn't a whole number.

13. Forgetting where the letters go

Wrong answer: 6760000 · Problem 15

26² × 10⁴ counts the letters and digits but not the 5 places the letter pair can sit.

14. Just short of a year

Wrong answer: 7 · Problem 16

7 mains give 14 × 7 × 3 = 294 dinners, fewer than 365. "Least" means the smallest that works, so check the one below too.

15. One checkerboard case

Wrong answer: 8192 · Problem 18

Putting the 1s on the light cells only. The 1s could be on the dark cells instead, which gives another 2¹² arrangements.

Worked problems

N = c1 × c2 × ⋯ × ck

Written the way a solution should be handed in: the narrowing reasoning first, the arithmetic last.

Problem 3: Exactly one yellow square

  1. What's hard? "Exactly one yellow." Coloring left to right, the number of options for a later square depends on whether yellow was already used, so there's no single multiplier.
  2. Reorder the decisions. Settle the hard rule first: which square is yellow.
  3. Count each decision. Yellow square: 5 choices. Each other square: green or blue, 2 choices, whatever came before.
  4. Multiply. 5 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 80.
  5. Check. A computer listing of all 3⁵ = 243 colorings finds 80 with one yellow.

Problem 8: Arranging GREENER

  1. Tally. Seven letters: E × 3, R × 2, G, N.
  2. Why not letter by letter? After an E there are 4 kinds of letter left; after G only 3. Uneven branches, no product.
  3. Pretend. Color the E's and R's so all seven letters are different: 7! = 5040 arrangements.
  4. How often is each real word counted? Once for each ordering of the colored E's (3! = 6) times each ordering of the colored R's (2! = 2): 12 times, the same for every word.
  5. Divide. 5040 ÷ 12 = 420.
Try it in practice

Problem 13: Candy for ten kids

  1. What's banned? Neighbor pairs totaling 4: (1, 3), (3, 1), (2, 2).
  2. Build left to right. First kid: 1, 2 or 3.
  3. Does the next kid always have the same number of options? After 1: 1 or 2. After 2: 1 or 3. After 3: 2 or 3. Different options, always two.
  4. Multiply. 3 × 2⁹ = 1536, since nine kids come after the first.
  5. Check. Brute force over all 3¹⁰ = 59,049 ways finds 1536.
Try it in practice

Problem 14: Middle digit is the average

  1. What's forced? Once the first and last digits are chosen, the middle digit is their average, if that's a whole number.
  2. When is it whole? When the first and last digits are both odd or both even.
  3. Count each kind. Both odd: 5 × 5 = 25. Both even, first not zero: 4 × 5 = 20.
  4. Add. 25 + 20 = 45. The two kinds never overlap.
  5. Check. A scan of 100–999 finds 45.
Try it in practice

Problem 18: The 0, 1, 2 grid

  1. Look at neighbors. Next to a 0 or a 2 must be a 1. Next to a 1 can be 0 or 2.
  2. Color the grid like a checkerboard. Neighbors are always opposite colors, so one color holds only 1s and the other holds only 0s and 2s.
  3. Count cells. With dark corners: 13 dark, 12 light.
  4. Two cases. Light all 1s: each of 13 dark cells is 0 or 2, 2¹³ = 8192. Dark all 1s: 2¹² = 4096.
  5. Add. 8192 + 4096 = 12,288. Check: a row-by-row computer count agrees, and the 3 × 3 version gives 2⁵ + 2⁴ = 48 by full listing.
Try it in practice

Glossary

0! = 1
Rule of product
If a process has steps with c₁, c₂, …, c_k options, and each step's number of options doesn't depend on earlier choices, the process can end c₁ × c₂ × ⋯ × c_k ways.
Permutation
An arrangement, or ordering, of a group of things.
Factorial, n!
n × (n − 1) × ⋯ × 1, the number of arrangements of n different objects.
0! and the empty product
0! = 1. There is exactly one way to arrange nothing. More generally, multiplying no numbers at all gives 1, the same way adding no numbers gives 0.
Distinct
Different from one another. "Distinct digits" means no digit repeats.
Identical
Impossible to tell apart. Swapping identical things doesn't make a new arrangement.
Overcounting
Counting some or all things more than once.
Factor of overcount
The number of times every object was counted, when it's the same for all of them. Divide by it.
Leading zero
A zero in the first place. 024 is the two-digit number 24, not a three-digit number.
Not necessarily distinct
Repeats are allowed but not required.
Ordered pair
(m, n) with order mattering: (1000, 492) and (492, 1000) are different pairs.
Set
A collection with no order. {A, B, C} is the same set as {C, A, B}.
Exactly one
One, no more and no fewer.
Neighbors (in a grid)
Cells sharing a side. Cells touching only at a corner are not neighbors.
Between 1000 and 9999
Usually means inclusive, but check whether the endpoints matter. For four distinct digits they don't: both repeat a digit.

Write-up habits

9 × 104 = 99,999 − 10,000 + 1

Drawn from how the teacher worked the problems in class.

  • Say what you're building. The class starts every count by naming the object and the decisions that make it: "a number is a list of digits, so decide each digit."
  • List the options for the first place or two. The teacher writes them out once ("1, 2, …, 9") and only then switches to counts.
  • Check a couple of sample paths. Choose A then C and see what's left; try a second path to make sure nothing behaves differently.
  • Show the restricted decision first. Write "which square is yellow: 5" before anything else.
  • Say why you divide. "Every word is counted 3! × 2! times" is the justification; the division is just arithmetic.
  • Leave big answers in factored form when the problem allows it: 11!/(4!·4!·2!), 3 × 2⁹. Cancel factorials before multiplying: 5!/3! = 5 × 4.
  • Check with a range or a small case. 99,999 − 10,000 + 1 = 90,000 confirmed 9 × 10⁴ in class.
