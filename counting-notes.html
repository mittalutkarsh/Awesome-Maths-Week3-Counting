From class

Outfits (Example 1.1). Four shirts, three pants, two shoes: 4 × 3 = 12 shirt-and-pants pairs, each with 2 shoe options, 24 outfits.

Worked numbers

A dinner is one of 10 appetizers, 5 mains and 3 desserts: 10 × 5 × 3 = 150 dinners. A three-digit number built left to right has 9 × 10 × 10 = 900 possibilities; built right to left it's 10 × 10 × 9, the same 900.

Columns can be decisions too. If m + n = 1492 with no carrying, each column of the sum splits on its own: the ones digit 2 is 0 + 2, 1 + 1 or 2 + 0, which is 3 ways. A digit d splits into d + 1 ordered pairs.

The pick grid

pairs = n × (n − 1)2

This is the picture to draw whenever a problem picks two things and you're not sure whether to divide. It comes straight from the group-of-2 problem on the unit test: Ms. Q splits Lizzie, Grogg, Alex, Winnie, Ralph and Fiona into a group of 2 and a group of 4.

Building the grid

  1. Set it up. Write the six students down the side (the first student picked) and again across the top (the second student picked). Every box is one way to say "first I pick this person, then I pick that person."
  2. How many boxes are there? 6 × 6 = 36.
  3. Why are the × boxes not allowed? You can't pick Lizzie and then pick Lizzie again. That removes the 6 boxes down the diagonal, leaving 30.
  4. This is the moment that matters. Point at the LG box, then at the GL box, and ask: are those two different groups? They aren't. It's Lizzie and Grogg either way. Every teal box has a grey twin on the other side of the diagonal, so the 30 boxes are really 15 groups, each showing up twice. 30 ÷ 2 = 15.

If he can answer step 4 himself, he understands the whole idea. That one question, "is this the same thing written backwards?", is what separates a problem where you divide from one where you multiply and stop.

Two surprises are worth saying out loud. First, the group of 4 costs nothing: if the problem had just said "choose 2 students" the grid would be exactly the same picture, and the answer still 15. Naming the 2 already names the 4. Second, you can check from the other side: choosing 4 of the 6 also gives 15, because every group of 4 leaves exactly one pair behind. The two counts list the same 15 splits from opposite ends.

Same grid, different answers

Draw the grid, then ask the three questions. The answers change from problem to problem, and so does what you divide by.

Home and away games: no twins

Problem 12 counts games between 8 teams who play each other twice, home and away. Handshakes: twins everywhere

One red card, one black card: no diagonal, no twins

When the two picks come from separate piles, the grid is a rectangle. No box picks the same card twice, and no box has a twin, because a red card can never be in the black column. Dividing by 2 here out of habit gives 338, which is wrong.

A spade and a face card: twins only in one corner

This is the hardest one, and the grid makes it easy to see. The two piles overlap in three cards, J♠, Q♠ and K♠. Only there can a box pick the same card twice (the three × boxes), and only there can two boxes be the same hand (J♠ + Q♠ appears once with J♠ as the spade, once with Q♠ as the spade). 156 − 3 − 3 = 150. The tempting wrong answers are 156 (no corrections) and 153 (only the × boxes).

Glossary

0! = 1
Rule of product
If a process has steps with c₁, c₂, …, c_k options, and each step's number of options doesn't depend on earlier choices, the process can end c₁ × c₂ × ⋯ × c_k ways.
Permutation
An arrangement, or ordering, of a group of things.
Factorial, n!
n × (n − 1) × ⋯ × 1, the number of arrangements of n different objects.
0! and the empty product
0! = 1. There is exactly one way to arrange nothing. More generally, multiplying no numbers at all gives 1, the same way adding no numbers gives 0.
Distinct
Different from one another. "Distinct digits" means no digit repeats.
Identical
Impossible to tell apart. Swapping identical things doesn't make a new arrangement.
Overcounting
Counting some or all things more than once.
Factor of overcount
The number of times every object was counted, when it's the same for all of them. Divide by it.
Leading zero
A zero in the first place. 024 is the two-digit number 24, not a three-digit number.
Not necessarily distinct
Repeats are allowed but not required.
Ordered pair
(m, n) with order mattering: (1000, 492) and (492, 1000) are different pairs.
Set
A collection with no order. {A, B, C} is the same set as {C, A, B}.
Exactly one
One, no more and no fewer.
Neighbors (in a grid)
Cells sharing a side. Cells touching only at a corner are not neighbors.
Between 1000 and 9999
Usually means inclusive, but check whether the endpoints matter. For four distinct digits they don't: both repeat a digit.

Write-up habits

9 × 104 = 99,999 − 10,000 + 1

Drawn from how the teacher worked the problems in class.

  • Say what you're building. The class starts every count by naming the object and the decisions that make it: "a number is a list of digits, so decide each digit."
  • List the options for the first place or two. The teacher writes them out once ("1, 2, …, 9") and only then switches to counts.
  • Check a couple of sample paths. Choose A then C and see what's left; try a second path to make sure nothing behaves differently.
  • Show the restricted decision first. Write "which square is yellow: 5" before anything else.
  • Say why you divide. "Every word is counted 3! × 2! times" is the justification; the division is just arithmetic.
  • Leave big answers in factored form when the problem allows it: 11!/(4!·4!·2!), 3 × 2⁹. Cancel factorials before multiplying: 5!/3! = 5 × 4.
  • Check with a range or a small case. 99,999 − 10,000 + 1 = 90,000 confirmed 9 × 10⁴ in class.
